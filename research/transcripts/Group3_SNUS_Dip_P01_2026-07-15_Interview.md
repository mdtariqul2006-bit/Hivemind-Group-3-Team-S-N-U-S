Work and recent onboarding

[03:50] Interviewer: Think about the last time you started a job or changed teams. What did the first few days or weeks actually look like?

[04:02] Participant P01: It was pretty quick, honestly. I had one induction day where HR ran through contracts and benefits, and then I was straight onto my team from day two. My manager gave me a folder of brand guidelines and client briefs and just said have a look through when you get a chance. There wasn't really a structured plan for the first week, I mostly figured things out as client work came in.

[05:20] Interviewer: In those first two weeks, what were the top three things you needed to know to actually do your job well?

[05:32] Participant P01: Probably first, understanding each client's brand voice, because that's different for every account. Second, knowing who on the team owned which client, so I wasn't stepping on anyone's toes. And third, learning our project management tool, because that's where everything lived and nobody really walked me through it properly.

[07:00] Interviewer: What was the hardest part of getting started, and why?

[07:12] Participant P01: Honestly, not knowing who to ask for what. Everyone was busy with their own clients, so I felt like I was interrupting people constantly in that first month, even for small things.

[08:40] Interviewer: Did the onboarding process feel helpful and relevant, or did it feel more like box-ticking? What made it feel that way?

[08:52] Participant P01: The HR side felt like box-ticking, lots of paperwork and policy documents. The actual team onboarding felt more useful, but it was really informal, it depended a lot on whoever had time to explain things to me that week.

Finding information at work

[10:10] Interviewer: When you need an answer at work, where do you go first?

[10:20] Participant P01: Usually Slack. I'll search old messages first or just ask in our team channel.

[10:35] Interviewer: And if you don't find it there?

[10:40] Participant P01: Then I check Notion, that's where we keep our client briefs and process docs, and if that fails I'll just message someone directly.

[11:30] Interviewer: How often do you have to check more than one place to find what you need, and what's annoying about that?

[11:40] Participant P01: Pretty often, maybe a few times a week. What's annoying is not knowing which version is the current one, sometimes Notion has an old brief that doesn't match what's actually been agreed with the client.

[12:50] Interviewer: Tell me about a time you couldn't find something important. What happened?

[13:02] Participant P01: There was a time I needed the latest brand guidelines for a client. I pulled up what I thought was the final version from Notion, but marketing had actually sent an updated one over email that never got uploaded. I ended up sending the client an asset with the wrong logo file, which was pretty embarrassing.

[14:20] Interviewer: How do you personally keep track of important links, documents or tasks? What's not great about your method?

[14:32] Participant P01: I keep a personal to-do list in Notion and I star important Slack messages, but it's messy. I've definitely lost track of things because they were buried in threads rather than saved somewhere central.

[15:50] Interviewer: If you had one home screen at work that showed the most important things, what would you want it to show?

[16:00] Participant P01: Honestly, my current tasks, deadlines coming up that week, and a quick way to see which client documents are the most recent version. Maybe also who's the go-to person for each client.

[17:10] Interviewer: What information should be very quick to reach, within a few clicks?

[17:18] Participant P01: Client contact details and the latest brand guidelines, definitely. Those are the things I need constantly and they shouldn't take five minutes to dig up.

[18:20] Interviewer: When you find a document or an answer, how do you know if it's actually correct or up to date?

[18:32] Participant P01: Honestly, most of the time I don't know for sure. I usually just check who last edited it and when, or message the account lead to double check before I send anything out to a client.

Starting a job & Smart Onboarding

[19:50] Interviewer: On your first day, what would have made you feel less lost?

[20:00] Participant P01: Probably just a simple list of what to actually do on day one. Log into which tools, meet which people, that kind of thing. I got a lot of documents to read but no clear starting point.

[21:10] Interviewer: In your first week, did you clearly know what to do next each day? If not, what was missing?

[21:22] Participant P01: Not really, no. After the induction day it was pretty unstructured, I was mostly just waiting for people to hand me things rather than having a clear plan.

[22:40] Interviewer: What makes onboarding feel personal and useful rather than generic?

[22:50] Participant P01: I think it's when someone actually tailors it to your role instead of giving you the same generic company deck everyone gets. Also just someone checking in on you specifically, not a mass email.

[24:00] Interviewer: What made you feel like you were doing okay or on track?

[24:12] Participant P01: Honestly, not much in the beginning. It was really just my manager saying things looked fine in our one-to-ones, there wasn't anything more concrete than that.

[25:20] Interviewer: When you needed help, did you know who to ask and how to get help quickly? What got in the way?

[25:32] Participant P01: Not at first, no. It took maybe three or four weeks before I actually knew who to go to for different things. Before that I was just guessing and hoping I wasn't bothering the wrong person.

AI helper trust

[26:50] Interviewer: Have you used AI tools at work — things like chatbots, AI search or coding assistants? What was good or bad about it?

[27:02] Participant P01: Yeah, we use ChatGPT quite a lot for first drafts of copy, and Notion AI for summarising meeting notes. The copy one is genuinely useful as a starting point. The meeting summaries are hit or miss though, sometimes it misses the actual decision that was made.

[28:10] Interviewer: If an AI tool gives you an answer at work, what would make you trust it?

[28:22] Participant P01: If it showed me where the information actually came from, like linking back to the specific doc or message, rather than just giving me an answer with no source.

[29:20] Interviewer: What would make you not trust it?

[29:30] Participant P01: If it gave me something confidently wrong, especially about a client detail, that would kill my trust in it pretty quickly. I'd double check everything after that.

[30:30] Interviewer: What kinds of workplace questions do you think an AI tool should answer with "you should ask a human" instead?

[30:42] Participant P01: Anything about pay, performance reviews, or anything sensitive like a client complaint. I wouldn't want an AI making a judgement call on that.

[31:50] Interviewer: Would you worry about privacy if an AI tool used internal documents or employee data? What would reassure you?

[32:02] Participant P01: A little, yeah, especially with client data. Knowing exactly what it can and can't see would help, and knowing there's always a person who can override it if it's wrong.
