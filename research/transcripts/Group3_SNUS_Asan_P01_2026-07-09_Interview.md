### Section B - Work and Recent Onboarding

[02:05] Interviewer: Think about the last time you started a job or changed team. What did the first days or weeks look like?

[02:14] Participant P01: So when I joined this company last year, day one was mostly HR paperwork and an IT setup that didn't fully work. My laptop arrived but half my accounts weren't provisioned, so I spent the afternoon emailing IT. Day two I had a welcome call with my manager, and then honestly… I was kind of left to it. I had a list of people to "say hi to" but no real structure. The first two weeks were a lot of sitting in meetings I didn't have context for and quietly Googling acronyms.

[03:05] Interviewer: Can you tell me more about that lack of structure? What did it feel like day to day?

[03:12] Participant P01: Every morning I'd open my laptop and think, "okay, what am I actually supposed to do today?" My manager was busy, so I didn't want to keep pinging her. I'd pick up small tasks people mentioned in passing, but I never knew if I was doing the right things or enough of them.

[03:38] Interviewer: In the first two weeks, what were the top three things you needed to know to do your job well?

[03:46] Participant P01: One, who owns what. Like who signs off creative, who runs the email platform, who I ask about budgets. Two, where the brand guidelines and campaign templates actually live, because there were three versions floating around. And three, how the weekly workflow runs. When briefs come in, when things are due, what the approval chain is.

[04:30] Interviewer: What was the hardest part of getting started, and why?

[04:36] Participant P01: Definitely finding information. Everything existed somewhere, but nothing was in one place. Some things were on SharePoint, some on Google Drive from before a migration, some only in people's heads. I felt like I was constantly interrupting people to ask things that should have been written down.

[05:10] Interviewer: Did onboarding feel helpful and relevant, or more like box-ticking? What made it feel that way?

[05:18] Participant P01: Box-ticking, honestly. There was a generic e-learning package (fire safety, GDPR, a company history video), the same for everyone whether you were in marketing or the warehouse. Nothing about my role. Once I clicked through those modules, onboarding was officially "done" from HR's point of view, but I didn't feel onboarded at all.

### Section C - Finding Information at Work

[06:00] Interviewer: When you need an answer at work, where do you go first?

[06:06] Participant P01: Slack, usually. I search old messages first, and if that fails I ask in the team channel. People are the real search engine at our company.

[06:22] Interviewer: How often do you have to check more than one place to find what you need? What's annoying about that?

[06:29] Participant P01: Most days, honestly. The annoying part is not knowing which version is current. I'll find a pricing doc on SharePoint and a slightly different one on Drive, and there's no way to tell which one is live. So I end up messaging someone to confirm anyway, which defeats the point of it being documented.

[07:05] Interviewer: Tell me about a time you couldn't find something important. What happened?

[07:11] Participant P01: In my first month I had to send a campaign brief to an agency and couldn't find the brief template. I searched everywhere, asked two colleagues who each pointed me to different outdated versions, and eventually the right one turned out to be pinned in a Slack channel I hadn't been added to. That cost me most of a day and I missed the internal deadline, which was mortifying as a new person.

[07:55] Interviewer: How do you personally keep track of important links, documents or tasks? What's not great about your method?

[08:03] Participant P01: I have a personal Notion page where I paste links, plus browser bookmarks, plus a notebook. The problem is it's all self-maintained. If a link changes or a doc moves, my system silently breaks and I don't find out until I need it.

[08:35] Interviewer: If you had one home screen at work showing the most important things, what would you want on it?

[08:42] Participant P01: My tasks for today with deadlines, key links that are guaranteed current, who to contact for what, and honestly some kind of "you're on track" indicator. In the first weeks I would have loved a progress view, like here's what you've done, here's what's next.

[09:15] Interviewer: What information should be very quick to reach, within a few clicks?

[09:21] Participant P01: Templates, brand guidelines, the org chart with responsibilities, holiday and expense policies, and how-to guides for our core tools.

[09:40] Interviewer: When you do find a document or answer, how do you know it's correct or up to date?

[09:46] Participant P01: Mostly I don't. I check the last-modified date and who edited it, but really I just ask someone senior to confirm. If documents had a clear "verified on this date by this owner" label, I'd trust them so much more.

### Section D - Starting a Job / Smart Onboarding

[10:30] Interviewer: On your first day, what would have made you feel less lost?

[10:36] Participant P01: A simple ordered checklist. "Do these five things today, in this order, and here's who to talk to if something breaks." Instead I had a welcome email with about fifteen links and no priority to any of them.

[11:05] Interviewer: In your first week, did you clearly know what to do next each day? If not, what was missing?

[11:12] Participant P01: No, not at all. What was missing was any kind of guided path. Even a rough plan (day one is setup, day two is meet these three people, day three is shadow this process) would have transformed it. I was reconstructing that plan myself by guesswork.

[11:45] Interviewer: What makes onboarding feel personal and useful rather than generic?

[11:52] Participant P01: When it's about your actual role and team. The generic compliance stuff is fine, it has to exist, but the useful part would be things like the tools your team uses, your first tasks, and how your manager likes to work. Also when a real human checks in on you, not just an automated survey after 30 days.

[12:35] Interviewer: What made you feel "I'm doing okay" or "I'm on track" (feedback, milestones, check-ins, visible progress)?

[12:43] Participant P01: The only real moment was a check-in with my manager at the end of week three where she said I was picking things up quickly. That one comment carried me for a month. Before that, silence, and as a new person you read silence as "maybe I'm failing." Regular small check-ins, or even a visible milestone list, would have helped massively.

[13:25] Interviewer: When you needed help, did you know who to ask and how to get help quickly? What got in the way?

[13:32] Participant P01: Not at first. The org chart just had names and titles, not what people actually own. And there's a social cost. You don't want to be the new person asking "stupid" questions publicly in a channel, so you either DM someone and worry you're bothering them, or you waste an hour searching alone.

### Section E - AI Helper Trust

[14:10] Interviewer: Have you used AI tools at work, like chatbots, AI search, or Copilot-style tools? What was good or bad about them?

[14:18] Participant P01: We have Copilot in Microsoft 365 and I use ChatGPT for drafting copy. The good part is speed for writing. The bad part is our internal chatbot pilot for HR questions. It confidently gave a colleague the wrong holiday carry-over policy. Once you see that happen, you stop trusting it for anything that matters.

[15:00] Interviewer: If an AI tool gives you an answer at work, what would make you trust it?

[15:06] Participant P01: Sources, definitely. Show me the exact document and section the answer came from, with the date it was last updated. If I can click through and verify in ten seconds, I'll use it constantly. If it's just a confident paragraph with no source, I have to double-check everything anyway.

[15:40] Interviewer: What would make you not trust it?

[15:44] Participant P01: No sources, out-of-date information, or being confidently wrong even once on something important. Also if it can't say "I don't know." A tool that always answers is scarier than one that sometimes says "ask a human."

[16:10] Interviewer: What kinds of workplace questions should an AI tool answer with "you should ask a human"?

[16:16] Participant P01: Anything sensitive, like pay, grievances, health stuff, conflicts with colleagues, or anything legal. And judgement calls, like "should I escalate this?" For those I want it to point me to the right person, not attempt an answer.

[16:50] Interviewer: Would you worry about privacy if an AI tool used internal documents or employee data? What would reassure you?

[16:58] Participant P01: A bit, yes. Mainly whether my questions are visible to my manager or HR. If I ask the bot about resignation notice periods, does that get flagged somewhere? What would reassure me is a clear, plain-English statement of what's logged, who can see it, and that personal queries aren't reported on individually.

### Section F - Wrap-up

[17:40] Interviewer: We're nearly done. What were the top two problems you mentioned today?

[17:47] Participant P01: First, not knowing what to do next in my first weeks, no guided path, no structure. Second, information being scattered across tools with no way to know what's current.

[18:10] Interviewer: What do those problems cost you, time, stress, mistakes, delays?

[18:16] Participant P01: All of them. I'd guess I lost several hours a week just hunting for things in my first couple of months. The missed deadline over the brief template was a direct mistake. And the stress was real. I nearly convinced myself I'd made a bad career move in week two, purely because I felt lost, not because the job was bad.

[18:50] Interviewer: Last one, if you could change one thing to make starting a job easier, what would it be?

[18:56] Participant P01: A personal, role-specific onboarding journey. Log in on day one and see your plan for today, this week, and this month. See your progress, your key documents that are guaranteed current, and who to ask about what. If that existed, the first month would have felt completely different.
